# Operators as modules and polynomial projections

## Put the operator into the scalar ring

Let $T:V\to V$ be $k$-linear. Define an action of $R=k[t]$ on $V$ by

$$p\cdot v=p(T)(v),\qquad
p(T)=a_0 1_V+a_1T+\cdots+a_dT^d
\quad\text{if }p(t)=\sum_{j=0}^d a_jt^j.$$

Polynomial evaluation is a unital $k$-algebra homomorphism
$k[t]\to\operatorname{End}_k(V)$, because the powers of a single operator
commute. Denote the resulting module by $V_T$.

For another operator $S:W\to W$, a map $f:V\to W$ is $k[t]$-linear
exactly when it is $k$-linear and

$$f\circ T=S\circ f.$$

The forward implication follows by applying $f$ to $t\cdot v$; the reverse
follows first for powers, then for polynomials. Thus operators and intertwining
maps form the same category as $k[t]$-modules whose underlying $k$-spaces
lie in the chosen dimension class. In particular, finite-dimensional operators
correspond to modules finite-dimensional over $k$, a smaller class than all
finitely generated $k[t]$-modules. Invariant subspaces are
submodules, invariant quotient operators are quotient modules, and similarity
means module isomorphism. This translation preserves the maps, not merely the
list of isomorphism classes.

If $n=\dim_k V<\infty$, a $k$-basis generates $V_T$ over $R$. Moreover,
$1_V,T,\ldots,T^{n^2}$ are linearly dependent in
$\operatorname{End}_k(V)$, so some nonzero polynomial annihilates $V_T$.
Its annihilator ideal has a unique monic generator $m_T$, the minimal
polynomial. For $V=0$, set $m_T=1$. This argument avoids using
Cayley–Hamilton before proving it.

## The PID structure theorem and its mechanism

**Structure theorem.** If $R$ is a principal ideal domain and $M$ is a finitely
generated $R$-module, then

$$M\cong R^r\oplus\bigoplus_{i=1}^s R/(d_i),
\qquad d_1\mid d_2\mid\cdots\mid d_s,$$

where the $d_i$ are nonzero nonunits. The free rank and the invariant-factor
ideals $(d_i)$ are unique. Over $k[t]$, choose the $d_i$ monic. The
isomorphism and its cyclic generators are generally not canonical.

Here is the construction in the Euclidean rings relevant to ordinary linear
algebra, $k[t]$ and $\mathbb Z$. Write $M$ as a cokernel of a matrix
$A:R^a\to R^b$. Such a finite presentation exists: a submodule of a
finite free module over a PID is finite free. Invertible row and column
operations change the bases of the target and source and preserve its cokernel.

If $A\ne0$, move a nonzero entry into the first position. Divide entries
in its column and row by this pivot. A nonzero remainder can be moved to the
pivot and has smaller Euclidean size. Repetition ends with a pivot dividing
its entire row and column, which can then be cleared. If the pivot fails to
divide an entry of the remaining block, add the corresponding row to the first
row; a subsequent column division produces a smaller nonzero pivot. Thus this
stage also terminates. The pivot now divides the remaining block. Repeat on that
block to obtain a diagonal matrix with $d_i\mid d_{i+1}$, including units
and zero entries as appropriate. Taking cokernels gives the theorem's existence
statement in these Euclidean cases.

For uniqueness, let $I_j(A)$ be the ideal generated by all $j\times j$
minors. These ideals are unchanged by invertible row or column operations;
multilinearity of minors gives containment in one direction and the inverse
operation gives the other. For Smith diagonal form,

$$I_j(A)=(d_1d_2\cdots d_j)$$

for the nonzero diagonal positions, including unit positions. Thus the
successive products, and hence the factors up to units, are determined. For
arbitrary module presentations, invariance of the corresponding presentation
ideals is a further theorem; alternatively uniqueness of the abstract
decomposition follows from primary filtrations below. We are not identifying
matrices of different presentation sizes without that extra argument.

For a general PID there need not be a Euclidean norm, so the specific descent
algorithm above is not a proof in that generality. Grillet VIII.6.1–6.3
(printed pp. 336–342, PDF pp. 348–354) gives the general proof using
principal ideals and a basis theorem. This is a cited extension of the
construction just explained.

**Why finite-dimensional operator modules have no free summand.** The
$k$-dimension of $k[t]$ is infinite, whereas $\dim_k V$ is finite. Hence

$$V_T\cong\bigoplus_{i=1}^s k[t]/(d_i),\qquad d_1\mid\cdots\mid d_s.$$

Every block $k[t]/(d)$ has basis $1,t,\ldots,t^{d_0-1}$ if
$d_0=\deg d>0$. Multiplication by $t$ has companion matrix

$$C(d)=\begin{pmatrix}
0&0&\cdots&0&-a_0\\
1&0&\cdots&0&-a_1\\
0&1&\cdots&0&-a_2\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\
0&0&\cdots&1&-a_{d_0-1}
\end{pmatrix},\quad
d(t)=t^{d_0}+a_{d_0-1}t^{d_0-1}+\cdots+a_0.
$$

The subdiagonal entries are all $1$ and unspecified entries are $0$;
for $d_0=1$ this is the one-entry matrix $(-a_0)$.
The defining relation $d(t)=0$ explains the last column. Expansion of
$\det(xI-C(d))$ along the last column gives $d(x)$; equivalently the
cofactor of its $j$th last-column entry is the appropriate power of $x$.
The invariant-factor blocks give the rational canonical form over any field.
Consequently

$$\chi_T=\prod_i d_i,\qquad m_T=d_s\quad(V\ne0).$$

In particular $m_T\mid\chi_T$, so $\chi_T(T)=0$. This derives
Cayley–Hamilton from the structure theorem, rather than assuming it in the
proof that the module is torsion. For $V=0$ the empty product is $1$.

There is also a useful presentation that makes contact with matrices:

$$V_T\cong\operatorname{coker}\bigl(tI-A:R^n\to R^n\bigr),$$

where $A$ is the matrix of $T$ in a chosen basis. Send a vector polynomial
$p(t)=\sum_j t^jv_j$ to $\sum_j A^jv_j$. The relation
$t v\equiv Av$ reduces every class modulo $(tI-A)R^n$ to a constant.
It also shows that a constant class maps to itself in $k^n$, so no nonzero
constant is killed. Thus the induced map is bijective. Computing Smith form of
$tI-A$ therefore computes similarity invariants of $A$; this is not the same
as applying arbitrary left and right equivalence to $A$ over $k$.

## Bézout and Chinese remainder projections

Suppose $f_1,\ldots,f_r\in k[t]$ are pairwise coprime and
$m=f_1\cdots f_r$ annihilates $T$. Set $g_i=m/f_i$. Bézout gives
$a_i g_i\equiv1\pmod{f_i}$. Let $e_i=a_i g_i$ in $k[t]/(m)$.
Its residues are $1$ modulo $f_i$ and $0$ modulo every other factor.
The Chinese remainder theorem, or divisibility by each coprime factor, gives

$$e_i^2=e_i,\qquad e_ie_j=0\ (i\ne j),\qquad\sum_i e_i=1
\quad\pmod m.$$

Evaluate at $T$ to obtain projections $P_i=e_i(T)$ satisfying these same
identities in $\operatorname{End}_k(V)$. Their images are exactly
$\ker f_i(T)$: first $f_i e_i$ is divisible by $m$, so the image lies
there; conversely $e_i-1$ is divisible by $f_i$, so $e_i(T)$ fixes that
kernel. Hence

$$V=\bigoplus_i\ker f_i(T).$$

The polynomials $a_i$ are not unique, but the projections are uniquely
determined by the specified factors and the action. If $m$ is the minimal
polynomial and the factors are its primary powers, these are the canonical
primary summands. Any operator commuting with $T$ commutes with all the $P_i$,
and thus preserves these summands.

**Small example.** If $T^2(T-1_V)=0$, take
$P_1=T^2$ and $P_0=1_V-T^2$. Indeed $T^3=T^2$ implies
$T^4=T^2$, so $P_1^2=P_1$, and

$$V=\ker T^2\oplus\ker(T-1_V).$$

With $T^2(T-1_V)^2=0$ instead, one may take

$$P_1=3T^2-2T^3,\qquad P_0=1_V-3T^2+2T^3.$$

These integer-coefficient formulas work in every characteristic: their defining
congruences are polynomial identities over $\mathbb Z$. There is no division
by $2$ or $3$. Only the distinctness of $0$ and $1$ is used.

**What fails without coprimality.** Take $R/(t^2)$. Its submodule
$tR/(t^2)$ and quotient $R/(t)$ are both one-dimensional over $k$.
They do not give an invariant direct sum: the only vectors killed by $t$ are
in $tR/(t^2)$. Dividing one repeated primary factor into smaller pieces
does not create Bézout projectors.

## Jordan form and what is actually canonical

Factor the invariant factors into monic irreducibles. Chinese remaindering gives
blocks $k[t]/(p^a)$. This elementary-divisor decomposition exists over every
field. If the minimal polynomial splits over $k$, each $p=t-\lambda$.
Writing $N=T-\lambda1_V$ on a block of size $a$, choose a cyclic vector
$v$ with $N^{a-1}v\ne0$ and $N^av=0$. The ordered basis

$$N^{a-1}v,N^{a-2}v,\ldots,Nv,v$$

gives the Jordan block $J_a(\lambda)$ with $1$ on the superdiagonal.
Thus Jordan form requires splitting of $m_T$ over $k$, not necessarily that
$k$ itself be algebraically closed.

To recover block sizes without choosing chains, put
$a_j=\dim\ker(T-\lambda1_V)^j$ and $a_0=0$.
Every $\lambda$-block of size $b$ contributes $\min(j,b)$; other primary
blocks contribute zero because $T-\lambda1_V$ is invertible there. Therefore

$$\begin{aligned}
a_j-a_{j-1}&=\#\{\lambda\text{-blocks of size at least }j\},\\
2a_j-a_{j-1}-a_{j+1}&=\#\{\lambda\text{-blocks of size exactly }j\}.
\end{aligned}$$

For example, nullities $0,3,5,6,6$ give three $\lambda$-blocks, two of size at least
$2$, one of size at least $3$, and none of size at least $4$: the sizes
are $3,2,1$. These are all the blocks if $\dim_kV=6$; otherwise another
primary summand can remain invisible to these kernels. Even saying that
$\lambda$ is the only eigenvalue in $k$ does not exclude a nonsplitting
summand. This gives uniqueness of the $\lambda$-block sizes and a practical
reconstruction procedure.
More generally a $p^b$-block contributes
$\deg(p)\min(j,b)$ to $\dim_k\ker p(T)^j$, so these filtrations recover
the elementary divisors over a nonsplitting field as well.

The primary subspaces and block-size multiset are intrinsic. Individual cyclic
summands, Jordan chains, their ordering, and a conjugating matrix are choices.
Conflating a canonical list of invariants with a canonical basis leads to many
false naturality claims.

**Diagonalizability and semisimplicity.** $T$ is diagonalizable over $k$ if
and only if $m_T$ is a product of distinct linear factors. The $k[t]$-module
$V_T$ is semisimple if and only if $m_T$ is a product of distinct monic
irreducible factors. These differ when the field does not split $m_T$.
A real plane rotation through a nonzero angle other than $\pi$ can be a
simple $\mathbb R[t]$-module without having a real eigenvector.

## Hom spaces and the commutant

A module map $R/(f)\to R/(g)$ is determined by the image $y$ of $1$,
and its well-definedness condition is $f y=0$ in $R/(g)$. For monic
$d=\gcd(f,g)$ write $f=d f_0$, $g=d g_0$ with
$\gcd(f_0,g_0)=1$. The condition $g\mid f y$ is equivalent to
$g_0\mid y$. Thus

$$\operatorname{Hom}_R(R/(f),R/(g))
\cong R/(d),\qquad h\bmod d\longmapsto
\bigl(1\longmapsto g_0h\bmod g\bigr).$$

Cancellation here takes place in the domain $k[t]$. In particular

$$\dim_k\operatorname{Hom}_R(R/(f),R/(g))=\deg\gcd(f,g).$$

The commutant of $T$ is
$\operatorname{End}_{k[t]}(V_T)$. If the elementary divisors are
$(t-\lambda)^{b_{\lambda,i}}$, then

$$\dim_k\{S:ST=TS\}
=\sum_\lambda\sum_{i,j}\min(b_{\lambda,i},b_{\lambda,j}).$$

This computes the number of independent commuting maps without solving all
matrix-entry equations. For a single cyclic block its commutant is $k[T]$.
For multiple identical blocks there are extra intertwiners, so a commuting
operator need not be a polynomial in $T$.

## Fitting decomposition and the boundary of the method

For $N\ge\dim V$, both the kernels and images of powers of $T$ have
stabilized. Then

$$V=\ker T^N\oplus\operatorname{im}T^N.$$

If $v=T^Nw$ lies in the intersection, then $T^{2N}w=0$;
stabilization gives $w\in\ker T^N$, so $v=0$. Rank–nullity gives
the direct sum. The restriction to the first summand is nilpotent and to the
second is bijective. These subspaces are canonical, though a basis for either
is not. This is the primary split into the factor $t$ and the factors coprime
to it, obtained without explicitly factoring the minimal polynomial.

For two commuting operators the ring is $k[s,t]$. It is not a PID: the ideal
$(s,t)$ is not principal. A simultaneous eigenbasis exists for a finite family
of commuting diagonalizable operators over a common splitting field: decompose
into eigenspaces of one operator and note that the others preserve them.
Their restrictions remain diagonalizable because their minimal polynomials
divide the original square-free split minimal polynomials, so induction applies.
Without diagonalizability, the one-variable module classification
does not provide simultaneous Jordan form.
