# Free algebras, exterior powers, determinants, and minors

## Free constructions remember their extension property

The tensor algebra of a vector space is

$$T(V)=\bigoplus_{r\ge0}V^{\otimes r},\qquad V^{\otimes0}=k,$$

with multiplication by concatenation of tensors. Each element has finitely many
nonzero degree components. The inclusions of $k$ and $V$ are part of the
construction. For any unital associative $k$-algebra $A$, a linear map
$f:V\to A$ extends uniquely to an algebra homomorphism

$$\widehat f:T(V)\to A,\qquad
\widehat f(v_1\otimes\cdots\otimes v_r)=f(v_1)\cdots f(v_r).$$

The product on the right is multilinear, giving a map on each tensor power;
concatenation proves multiplicativity, and generation by $V$ proves uniqueness.
An algebra generated by a specified copy or image of $V$ is therefore a quotient
of $T(V)$ by its relations. The quotient has to impose exactly the intended
identities; it cannot be guessed from a superficial resemblance of formulas.

The symmetric algebra is

$$\operatorname{Sym}(V)=T(V)/\langle v\otimes w-w\otimes v:v,w\in V\rangle.$$

It is the free **commutative** unital algebra on $V$. The extension above
factors precisely when the images $f(v)$ commute. With a basis
$e_1,\ldots,e_n$, sending $e_i$ to a variable $x_i$ identifies it with
$k[x_1,\ldots,x_n]$. The variables and monomial basis depend on the chosen
basis, while the free property does not.

The homogeneous degree-$r$ part $\operatorname{Sym}^r(V)$ represents
symmetric $r$-linear maps from $V^r$. Notice the variance: polynomial
expressions on a space $V$ belong to $\operatorname{Sym}(V^*)$, not
$\operatorname{Sym}(V)$, once its vectors are regarded as points.
Over a finite field, formal polynomials are not determined by their values on
all points: $x^q-x$ vanishes on $\mathbb F_q$ but is a nonzero polynomial.
Thus a coordinate ring should not silently be replaced by the ring of set
functions on its rational points.

## Exterior algebra: alternation is the relation

Define

$$\Lambda(V)=T(V)/\langle v\otimes v:v\in V\rangle.$$

The product is denoted $\wedge$. Expanding $(v+w)\wedge(v+w)=0$
gives $v\wedge w=-w\wedge v$, and exchanging blocks gives
$\alpha\wedge\beta=(-1)^{rs}\beta\wedge\alpha$ for homogeneous
degrees $r,s$. The defining equation $v\wedge v=0$ remains necessary
in characteristic $2$: a sign rule alone does not imply it there.

The degree-$r$ piece $\Lambda^r(V)$ represents alternating $r$-linear
maps: an alternating map vanishes whenever two arguments are equal. Its unique
factorization through $V^{\otimes r}$ kills the repeated-vector relations;
conversely the wedge map is alternating. This construction works over any
commutative ring, not only in characteristic zero.

With an ordered basis $e_1,\ldots,e_n$, the wedges

$$e_{i_1}\wedge\cdots\wedge e_{i_r},\qquad
1\le i_1<\cdots<i_r\le n$$

form a basis. Reordering and removing repetitions proves spanning. For
independence, for each ordered subset $I=(i_1,\ldots,i_r)$ define on
vectors with coordinates $v_{ab}$ the alternating coefficient form

$$\beta_I(v_1,\ldots,v_r)
=\sum_{\sigma\in S_r}\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)
\prod_{a=1}^r v_{i_a,\sigma(a)}.$$

When two columns coincide, pair the permutations exchanging those columns;
their terms sum to zero, in characteristic $2$ as well. Thus $\beta_I$
factors through $\Lambda^r(V)$ and takes value $1$ on the $I$ basis
wedge and $0$ on the other ascending ones. This proves independence without
dividing by $r!$. Therefore

$$\dim\Lambda^r(V)=\binom nr,\qquad\Lambda^r(V)=0\ (r>n).$$

For $r=0$, $\Lambda^0(V)=k$. The zero-dimensional top exterior power is
therefore a line too.

## Antisymmetrizing tensors is a model with extra hypotheses

If $r!$ is invertible in $k$, the operator

$$\operatorname{Alt}_r
=\frac1{r!}\sum_{\sigma\in S_r}\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)\sigma
:V^{\otimes r}\to V^{\otimes r}$$

is idempotent. Its image identifies with $\Lambda^r(V)$: the quotient
map restricted to that image is inverse to the map induced by
$\operatorname{Alt}_r$. With this normalization, exterior multiplication in
the image is $\alpha\wedge\beta=\operatorname{Alt}_{r+s}(\alpha\otimes\beta)$.
Other common conventions place factorial factors in the identification, so
one must check which model a formula uses.

The analogous averaging operator without signs models symmetric powers when
$r!$ is invertible. In small characteristic the quotient definitions remain
valid but these normalized projectors do not. Symmetric powers (coinvariants)
and invariant symmetric tensors must not be identified using a nonexistent
$1/r!$. Polarization formulas with factorial denominators have the same
limitation. Xi II §6.4 explicitly assumes characteristic zero for its
antisymmetric-tensor model (printed p. 292, PDF p. 298); Grillet XIII.4 uses
the quotient construction over a commutative ring (printed pp. 523–527,
PDF pp. 535–539). These are related constructions with different stated scope.

## Functoriality, rank, and determinants

For $f:V\to W$, the alternating map
$(v_1,\ldots,v_r)\mapsto f(v_1)\wedge\cdots\wedge f(v_r)$ induces

$$\Lambda^r f:\Lambda^r V\to\Lambda^r W.$$

Checking decomposable wedges gives
$\Lambda^r(g\circ f)=\Lambda^r g\circ\Lambda^r f$ and
$\Lambda^r1_V=1_{\Lambda^rV}$. For $r>0$,

$$\Lambda^r f\ne0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\operatorname{rank}f\ge r.$$

If the image has dimension less than $r$, every image wedge is zero. If
the rank is at least $r$, choose $r$ vectors whose images are independent
and extend those images to a basis of $W$; their wedge is nonzero by the
basis theorem. This is the intrinsic meaning of testing rank by minors.

For $n=\dim V$ define the determinant line
$\det V=\Lambda^nV$. It is one-dimensional. An endomorphism $T:V\to V$
therefore acts on it by a unique scalar:

$$\Lambda^n T=(\det T)1_{\det V}.$$

This scalar is the determinant. Functoriality immediately gives
$\det(ST)=\det S\det T$, and $\det T\ne0$ precisely when $T$ is
invertible. For $V=0$ the determinant is $1$ by the degree-zero convention.
Expanding $T(e_1)\wedge\cdots\wedge T(e_n)$ gives the usual signed
permutation formula. Thus elimination formulas can be reconstructed from
alternation and the chosen volume basis.

If $f:V\to W$ maps two different $n$-dimensional spaces, it determines a
map $\det V\to\det W$. There is no preferred scalar determinant until
trivializations of both lines are chosen. This distinction is useful in
coordinate changes, orientation, and families of vector spaces.

## Minors and Cauchy–Binet from composition

Let $A$ be the matrix of $f:k^n\to k^m$. In ascending wedge bases, the
entry of $\Lambda^r f$ in row subset $I$ and column subset $J$ is the
minor $\det A_{I,J}$. This follows by expanding the image of
$e_{j_1}\wedge\cdots\wedge e_{j_r}$ and collecting the $I$ coefficient.

For a second matrix $B:k^m\to k^\ell$, functoriality gives

$$\det(BA)_{I,J}
=\sum_{\substack{K\subseteq\{1,\ldots,m\}\\|K|=r}}
\det B_{I,K}\det A_{K,J}.$$

This is the full minors version of Cauchy–Binet. The familiar determinant
formula for a rectangular product is the case $r=n=\ell$. The same proof
works over commutative rings using finite free modules and the exterior basis
theorem. A rank interpretation over a field should not be carried unchanged
to rings with zero divisors.

Expanding $\det(xI-T)$ by the columns in which $-T$ is selected gives

$$\chi_T(x)=\sum_{r=0}^n(-1)^r
\operatorname{tr}(\Lambda^rT)x^{n-r}.$$

The trace of $\Lambda^rT$ is the sum of principal $r$-minors. In
particular $\operatorname{tr}T$ and $\det T$ are the first and last
nontrivial elementary symmetric coefficients. This works without eigenvalues
in $k$. In positive characteristic, recovering these coefficients from power
traces via Newton identities can require division by a nonunit integer; the
exterior-power formula itself does not.

## Determinant lines of exact sequences

For a short exact sequence of finite-dimensional vector spaces
$0\to A\xrightarrow{i}B\xrightarrow{p}C\to0$, put
$a=\dim A$ and $c=\dim C$. There is a canonical isomorphism

$$\det A\otimes\det C\xrightarrow{\ \sim\ }\det B.$$

Send a decomposable tensor to

$$(a_1\wedge\cdots\wedge a_a)\otimes(c_1\wedge\cdots\wedge c_c)
\longmapsto
i(a_1)\wedge\cdots\wedge i(a_a)
\wedge\widetilde c_1\wedge\cdots\wedge\widetilde c_c,$$

where $p(\widetilde c_j)=c_j$. Changing a lift adds a vector of $i(A)$,
whose extra occurrence makes that term zero. Multilinearity and alternation
give a map on the determinant lines, and lifting a basis of $C$ gives a basis
of $B$, so it is an isomorphism. It is independent of the chosen lifts,
although the splitting of the original sequence is not. The order “$A$ then
$C$” is part of the convention; reversing it contributes $(-1)^{ac}$.

An operator preserving $A$ therefore satisfies
$\det T_B=\det T_A\det T_C$. Applied to $xI-T$ over $k(x)$, or directly
to its block triangular matrix, this gives
$\chi_{T_B}=\chi_{T_A}\chi_{T_C}$ even without an invariant complement.
Multiplication of invariants across an exact sequence does not imply splitting.

## Contraction and reconstruction

For $\lambda\in V^*$ and $r\ge1$ define contraction
$\iota_\lambda:\Lambda^rV\to\Lambda^{r-1}V$ by

$$\iota_\lambda(v_1\wedge\cdots\wedge v_r)
=\sum_{j=1}^r(-1)^{j-1}\lambda(v_j)
v_1\wedge\cdots\widehat v_j\cdots\wedge v_r.$$

The right side is alternating and multilinear in the $v_j$, so the
universal property makes it well defined. Set contraction on $\Lambda^0V=k$
to zero; together these maps define a degree $-1$ operator on the exterior
algebra. Splitting the sum into the positions
of $\alpha$ and $\beta$ gives

$$\iota_\lambda(\alpha\wedge\beta)
=(\iota_\lambda\alpha)\wedge\beta
+(-1)^r\alpha\wedge\iota_\lambda\beta
\quad(\deg\alpha=r).$$

This supplies a controlled introduction to signs in graded constructions.
Exterior powers, contractions, and determinant lines are useful now; differential
forms additionally require a manifold and smooth variation. They should not
be identified merely because the same wedge symbol appears.

The core memory is short: free algebra extends linear maps, quotient imposes
relations, exterior power transports alternating data, and the top exterior
power is a line. Minors, rank tests, determinant multiplication, and
Cauchy–Binet can be rebuilt from those principles.
