# Compression, orthogonal ideals, and a common-lift obstruction

The earlier Hadamard chapter proves that compression preserves correlation
equations. Here we determine exactly what it retains, count the individual
sign lifts, and explain the fixed core-nine failure by one polynomial
identity. These are three different tasks: lifting each row, satisfying a
quotient equation, and lifting all rows so that their full equations hold.
Their separation is useful even when every underlying algebra is semisimple.

## The quotient has an explicit orthogonal splitting

Write $m=dr$ with $d,r$ positive integers, and work over the reals:

$$A=\mathbb R[z]/(z^m-1),\qquad
B=\mathbb R[w]/(w^d-1),\qquad q(z)=w.$$

The involution is $z^*=z^{-1}$. Put

$$e=\frac1r\sum_{\ell=0}^{r-1}z^{\ell d}.$$

Each element of the subgroup generated by $z^d$ occurs $r$ times in
the square of the numerator, so $e^2=e$. Negation permutes that subgroup,
so $e^*=e$, and $q(e)=1$. Also $ez^d=e$. It follows that

$$\iota:B\longrightarrow eA,\qquad \iota(w^s)=ez^s$$

is well defined and is inverse to $q|_{eA}$. The identity of $eA$ is $e$,
not $1_A$. Since $e(1-e)=0$, multiplication gives the algebra decomposition

$$A=eA\oplus(1-e)A,\qquad \ker q=(1-e)A.$$

It is also orthogonal for the coefficient inner product
$\langle X,Y\rangle=[1](XY^*)$. Indeed,
$\langle eX,(1-e)Y\rangle=[1](e(1-e)XY^*)=0$.

If $X=\sum_jx_jz^j$ and $y_s=\sum_{j\equiv s\pmod d}x_j$, direct
coefficient collection gives

$$(eX)_j=\frac{y_{j\bmod d}}r,\qquad
\|eX\|^2=\frac1r\sum_{s=0}^{d-1}y_s^2.$$

The retained component $eX$ is the mean on each fiber; compression records
$r$ times those means. The remaining coefficients record deviations from
them; they are not arbitrary once signs and symmetry are imposed.

For four rows put $L_i=eX_i$ and $R_i=(1-e)X_i$. Cross products vanish,
so the full Gram identity $\sum_iX_iX_i^*=4m1_A$ is equivalent to two
independent algebra equations:

$$\sum_iL_iL_i^*=4me,\qquad
\sum_iR_iR_i^*=4m(1-e).$$

The first is precisely $\sum_iq(X_i)q(X_i)^*=4m$ in $B$.
There is no extra factor of $r$ in the right side. Taking the coefficient
of the identity gives total energies $4d$ and $4(m-d)$ respectively.
For example, the first follows from
$\sum_{i,s}y_{i,s}^2=4m$ and the factor $1/r$ above.

The algebraic decomposition holds for all positive $d,r$. Oddness,
normalization, and signs enter the following discrete problem.

## The exact count of normalized symmetric row lifts

Assume now that $m,d,r$ are odd. A normalized symmetric sign row satisfies

$$x_j\in\{-1,1\},\qquad x_{-j}=x_j,\qquad x_0=1.$$

An integer row $y\in\mathbb Z^d$ has such a lift if and only if:

1. $y_s=y_{-s}$ for all $s$;
2. for $s\ne0$, $|y_s|\le r$ and $y_s\equiv r\pmod2$;
3. $y_0=r-4a$ for an integer $0\le a\le(r-1)/2$.

When these conditions hold, the number of lifts is

$$
\binom{(r-1)/2}{(r-y_0)/4}
\prod_{s=1}^{(d-1)/2}\binom r{(r-y_s)/2}.
$$

**Proof.** Negation exchanges the two nonzero fibers $F_s$ and $F_{-s}$.
Choose $(r-y_s)/2$ negative positions among the $r$ positions of $F_s$;
reflection then fixes the entire opposite fiber. Different pairs of fibers
are independent. The zero fiber contains the fixed coordinate $0$, with
value $1$, and $(r-1)/2$ opposite pairs. Making $a$ pairs negative changes
its all-positive sum $r$ by $4a$. This proves both necessity and sufficiency,
as well as the product count.

Notice that the smallest possible zero-fiber sum is $2-r$, not $-r$.
For $d=1$ the product is empty; for $r=1$ the conditions say exactly that
$y$ itself is the unique normalized symmetric sign row.

This proof gives explicit reconstruction. Choose the negative-position
subsets on representative fibers, reflect them, and independently choose
the negative pairs on the zero fiber. It does not solve the equations that
couple the four rows.

## What remains after subtracting the fiber means

On a nonzero representative fiber, write $x=\mathbf1-2u$, where
$u\in\{0,1\}^r$ and $\sum u_j=b=(r-y_s)/2$. Then

$$x-\frac{y_s}{r}\mathbf1
 =-2\left(u-\frac br\mathbf1\right).$$

These are the translated and rescaled vertices of the hypersimplex

$$\Delta(r,b)=\{u\in[0,1]^r:\ \sum u_j=b\}.$$

The opposite fiber is determined by reflection, not chosen independently.
The zero fiber has one fixed coordinate and duplicated opposite pairs.
Its choices are an affine image of the vertices of
$\Delta((r-1)/2,a)$; treating it as an unrestricted
$\Delta(r,(r-y_0)/2)$ would count forbidden rows.
Explicitly, if $u$ records the $a$ negative pairs, the residual coordinate
at zero is $4a/r$, and both coordinates of pair $j$ are $4a/r-2u_j$.
Use $\Delta(0,0)=\{()\}$ when $r=1$.

Consequently the unresolved equation
$\sum_iR_iR_i^*=4m(1-e)$ is imposed on a finite product of specified
vertex sets. Real positivity alone does not enforce those sets. Nor does
the existence of an individual lift for every compressed row guarantee
a simultaneous solution.

There is a particularly transparent real relaxation. Suppose real
compressed rows $Y_i\in B$ already satisfy $\sum_iY_iY_i^*=4m$.
Set $L_i=\iota(Y_i)$, choose

$$R_1=2\sqrt m(1-e),\qquad R_2=R_3=R_4=0,$$

and put $X_i=L_i+R_i$. Then $q(X_i)=Y_i$ and
$\sum_iX_iX_i^*=4m$, because
$R_1R_1^*=4m(1-e)$. If the $Y_i$ are symmetric, the $X_i$ are
symmetric too. This proves existence of an unrestricted real lift, not
of normalized sign rows. It explains why an obstruction can disappear
completely after replacing a discrete coefficient problem by a real
algebra problem.

## A proof of the fixed core-nine obstruction

Consider the four compressed rows, with labels fixed in this order,

$$
Y_A=(-1,-1,-1),\quad Y_B=(-1,1,1),\quad
Y_C=(-1,3,3),\quad Y_D=(3,-1,-1).
$$

They satisfy the compressed Gram equation with target $36$.
The lift-count formula gives $3,3,1,3$ individual lifts, hence $27$
labeled four-row lifts. Since the compressed rows are distinct, there
are also $27$ unordered four-row multisets; if only their compressed
multiset is prescribed and row order matters, there are
$24\cdot27=648$ ordered tuples. These are different counting conventions.

A normalized symmetric row of length nine is determined by positions
$1,2,3,4$, and its compression is

$$y_0=1+2x_3,\qquad y_1=y_2=x_1+x_2+x_4.$$

Let $P=\{1,2,4\}$. On $P$, rows $A,D$ each have one positive entry,
row $B$ has one negative entry, and row $C$ is entirely positive.
At position $3$ the column is $(-1,-1,-1,1)^{\mathsf T}$.
Write $p,q$ for the positive exceptions of $A,D$, and $k$ for
the negative exception of $B$.

The odd Williamson product theorem, proved in the earlier chapter,
requires the product of every nonzero column to be $-1$. If $p=q$,
the product of $A,D$ is positive everywhere on $P$, which would require
$B$ to be negative everywhere there. If $p\ne q$, that product is
negative at $p,q$ and positive at the remaining position. Thus the
product condition holds exactly when $p,q,k$ are the three different
elements of $P$. It rejects $21$ lifts and leaves six.

All six fail for the same algebraic reason. In $\mathbb Z[z]/(z^9-1)$
put $S_j=z^j+z^{-j}$. Let $\mathbf1\in\mathbb Z^4$ and
$v_i=\mathbf1-2e_i$, with labels $i=A,B,C,D$. Then

$$v_i\cdot v_j=4[i=j],\qquad \mathbf1\cdot v_i=2.$$

For the surviving pattern, the columns at $p,q,k,3$ are respectively
$v_D,v_A,-v_C,-v_D$. Hence the vector of four row polynomials is

$$\mathbf X=\mathbf1+v_DS_p+v_AS_q-v_CS_k-v_DS_3.$$

Since each $X_i^*=X_i$ by symmetry, taking its dot product with itself
gives the relevant Gram polynomial:

$$
\sum_iX_i^2
=4+4(S_p+S_q-S_k-S_3)
 +4\sum_{j=1}^4S_j^2-8S_pS_3.
$$

Doubling permutes the four nonzero opposite-index classes modulo nine,
so

$$\sum_{j=1}^4S_j^2=8+S_p+S_q+S_k+S_3.$$

For $p\in\{1,2,4\}$, the two classes represented by $3-p,3+p$
are exactly the other two members $q,k$ of $P$. Therefore
$S_pS_3=S_q+S_k$, and substitution gives the precise defect

$$\boxed{\ \sum_iX_i^2-36=8(S_p-S_k)\ne0.\ }$$

The supports of $S_p$ and $S_k$ are disjoint. The summed
autocorrelation is $8$ at shifts $\pm p$, $-8$ at shifts $\pm k$,
and zero at all other nonzero shifts. Compression kills this defect:
$q(S_p)=q(S_k)=w+w^{-1}$. Thus the remaining obstruction lies
exactly in the complementary ideal discarded by the quotient.

This proves that none of the $27$ lifts in the specified normalized
symmetric family works. The [exact checker](../scripts/check_lifting.py)
independently reconstructs
the rows and their correlations, including all six polynomial defects.
It is a bounded verification of this proof and fixture, not a proof
about all Hadamard matrices of order $36$ or a new enumeration campaign.
The odd-product theorem is classical; see Curtis Bright,
[“A New Form of Williamson's Product Theorem,” Theorem 1](https://arxiv.org/abs/1711.07056).
No literature-priority claim is made for the explanatory calculation.

## What the obstruction suggests, and what it does not establish

Compression gives exact necessary equations and an explicit real
splitting. Integral Fourier reconstruction gives exact coefficient
lattice conditions. The affine-lattice sign theorem in the preceding
chapter combines those conditions with parity and energy. Each is a
complete statement about its specified data. None proves that feasible
partial data have a common completion.

For an algorithm, the remaining question is operational: can the
constraints be represented and propagated more cheaply than retaining
all original sign variables? Rewriting a condition as an exact lattice
membership test does not answer that question. A useful next experiment
would compare two implementations on the same fixed fixtures while
recording time, memory, and rejected states. That experiment is outside
this foundation study; no improvement in search complexity is claimed.

## Reconstruction exercises with solutions

**1. Recover the scale.** For $m=15,d=5$, a compressed four-row solution
has total coefficient energy $60$. Find the total energies of its
retained and complementary components.

**Solution.** Here $r=3$. Fiber averaging divides the retained energy
by three, giving $20=4d$; the complementary target is
$60-20=40=4(m-d)$. The quotient Gram target remains $60$.

**2. Detect a forbidden zero fiber.** Can a normalized symmetric sign
row with fiber size five have compressed zero coordinate $-5$, $-3$,
or $1$?

**Solution.** The possible zero sums are $5-4a$ for $a=0,1,2$,
namely $5,1,-3$. Thus $-5$ is forbidden even though it has the
correct ordinary sign-sum parity and bound.

**3. Explain a false converse.** Every compressed row in the core-nine
fixture has a sign lift and the compressed Gram equation holds.
Which step fails in inferring a Williamson quadruple?

**Solution.** Individual choices must also solve a common equation in
$(1-e)A$. The product theorem leaves six choices, each with nonzero
defect $8(S_p-S_k)$ in that ideal. Neither individual feasibility nor
the quotient equation couples those choices adequately.

**4. Separate algebra and arithmetic.** Why does the real lift above
not contradict the no-sign-lift proof?

**Solution.** It imposes neither the finite fiber vertex sets nor the
fixed coefficient $x_0=1$. The real group algebra splits by an
idempotent containing $1/r$; the normalized integral sign domain is
not the product of unrestricted real subsets in its two ideals.
